The Virginia Supreme Court on Friday struck down the congressional redistricting referendum voters approved in late April, voiding a map that would have given Democrats an advantage in 10 of the state's 11 U.S. House seats and that the party had counted on to offset Republican redraws across the South.

The ruling, in Scott v. McDougle, lands less than 24 hours after Tennessee Gov. Bill Lee signed a new map dissolving the state's only Black-majority district, and the same day Alabama Republicans approved their own redraw. With Virginia blocked, the cycle that began with Texas in 2025 and California's response under Gov. Gavin Newsom is breaking in Republicans' favor before November.

## What the court held

Writing for the majority, Justice D. Arthur Kelsey said Virginia had "submitted a proposed constitutional amendment to Virginia voters in an unprecedented manner that violated the intervening-election requirement." Under Article XII of the state constitution, the General Assembly must pass an amendment, hold a regularly scheduled House of Delegates election, and pass it again before sending it to voters.

The legislature voted on the proposal Oct. 31, 2025, after early voting in that fall's House of Delegates race had begun Sept. 19. By Oct. 31, CBS News reported, more than 1.3 million Virginians, roughly 40 percent of the electorate, had already cast ballots. The court ruled those voters were deprived of the chance to elect the legislature that would take the amendment's second vote, a defect that, in the majority's words, "irreparably undermines the integrity of the resulting referendum vote" and renders it void. The referendum had passed by three percentage points in late April.

## What it costs Democrats

Virginia's House delegation splits six Democrats to five Republicans. The blocked map would have given Democrats up to four additional seats, PBS NewsHour reported; under the existing lines, David Wasserman of The Cook Political Report With Amy Walter told PBS, Democrats can still pick up one or two.

Nationally, Wasserman estimated Republicans are positioned to net roughly six or seven seats from the current redistricting cycle. An Issue One analysis cited by CNBC put the combined Republican edge at as much as 12 seats with Virginia off the board. Louisiana has paused its primaries to redraw, and South Carolina House Republicans have proposed a new map.

## The reaction

President Trump, who has pressed GOP-led states to redraw maps to defend a slim House majority, wrote on Truth Social that the ruling was a "Huge win for the Republican Party, and America, in Virginia." House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., called it "a victory for democracy" that ensures "fair representation in Congress" for Virginians. Virginia Senate Republican Leader Ryan T. McDougle, the named defendant, said the ruling "affirms what we all know: you cannot violate the Constitution to change the Constitution."

Virginia House Speaker Don Scott, a Democrat, said he respected the court but would "keep fighting for a democracy where voters — not politicians — have the final say." Scott's attorneys said in a Friday evening filing they would seek an emergency appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries said Democrats are "exploring all options to overturn this shocking decision."

## The Republican case

The substantive Republican objection, adopted by the court, is procedural: Democrats moved on the amendment days before the legislative election the state constitution treats as an intervening check, and doing so during early voting voided the process. CNBC reported the court rejected the Democratic argument that the three-point margin at the ballot box cured the defect.

Democratic state senators separately accused the Trump administration of political prosecution after FBI agents raided the Portsmouth office of Sen. L. Louise Lucas on Friday in a federal corruption probe. Fox News reported, citing The Associated Press, that the investigation began under former President Joe Biden. Lucas has not been charged.

## What is next

Democrats have signaled they will run on the existing map while litigating. Rep. Steve Cohen, the Memphis Democrat whose seat Tennessee's new map would eliminate, has vowed to sue. Wasserman told PBS the landscape is "shifting at a dizzying pace" and that blue states are likely to respond with maximalist maps in 2028.
