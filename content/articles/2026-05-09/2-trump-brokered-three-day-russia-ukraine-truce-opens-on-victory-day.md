A three-day ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine brokered by President Trump took effect Saturday morning as Russian President Vladimir Putin presided over a scaled-back Victory Day parade on Red Square that for the first time included North Korean troops and rolled past spectators without tanks or missiles.

The truce, running May 9 through 11, paired a halt in fighting with an exchange of 1,000 prisoners of war by each side, the most concrete step toward de-escalation in a war now in its fifth year. It supersedes a 72-hour Kremlin truce and a 30-day offer from Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy that both collapsed earlier in the week, with each side blaming the other for continuing attacks.

## How it came together

Trump announced the agreement Friday on social media and confirmed it to reporters as he left the White House. "I asked and, President Putin agreed. President Zelenskyy agreed -- both readily," Trump said. "And we have a little period of time where they're not going to be killing people. That's very good." He called the pause potentially the "beginning of the end of a very long, deadly, and hard fought War."

Kremlin foreign affairs adviser Yuri Ushakov confirmed Russia's acceptance, and Zelenskyy publicly endorsed the deal, saying Ukraine's priority was retrieving prisoners of war. "Red Square matters less to us than the lives of Ukrainian prisoners of war who can be brought home," Zelenskyy wrote. He issued a presidential decree authorizing Saturday's parade and declaring Red Square temporarily off-limits for Ukrainian strikes, then asked Washington for assurances that Russia will honor the terms.

## On Red Square

Putin used his speech to frame the war in NATO terms, telling assembled troops that Russian forces in Ukraine "face an aggressive force that is armed and supported by the entire bloc of NATO." "Victory has always been and will be ours," Putin said. "The key to success is our moral strength, courage and valor, our unity and ability to endure anything and overcome any challenge."

The 45-minute parade was the first in nearly two decades to proceed without tanks, missiles or other heavy weapons, aside from a flyover of combat jets. In place of armor, organizers played video of Russian equipment deployed to Ukraine. Officials cited the "current operational situation" and the threat of Ukrainian drone strikes, and Moscow ordered restrictions on mobile internet and text messaging across the capital.

North Korean soldiers marched alongside Russian columns for the first time, a public acknowledgment of Pyongyang's role in helping Moscow repel a Ukrainian incursion into Russia's Kursk region. Foreign attendees included Belarusian leader Alexander Lukashenko and the presidents of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. Slovak Prime Minister Robert Fico met Putin separately but skipped the parade.

## What is at stake

Prior pauses have not held. Russia's larger force has been making slow gains along a front line of more than 1,000 kilometers, while Ukraine has hit back with drones now capable of reaching targets more than 1,000 kilometers inside Russia, striking energy facilities, plants and military depots. A working three-day cessation, paired with a 2,000-person prisoner exchange, would be the broadest verifiable step Trump's mediation has produced.

## The skeptics

The view from Moscow was less conciliatory than the choreography suggested. Russian authorities had warned that any Ukrainian attempt to disrupt Saturday's festivities would draw a "massive missile strike on the center of Kyiv," with the Defense Ministry urging civilians and foreign diplomats to leave the Ukrainian capital; the European Union said its diplomats would stay. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov dismissed Zelenskyy's decree authorizing the parade as a "silly joke," telling reporters, "We don't need anyone's permission to be proud of our Victory Day." Secretary of State Marco Rubio, speaking before Trump's announcement, told reporters that broader diplomatic efforts "have stagnated" despite U.S. willingness to assist, a caveat that frames the May 9-11 window less as a breakthrough than a holiday-timed pause.

The ceasefire is scheduled to expire at the end of Monday. By then, Trump has said, the prisoner exchange should be complete, and Washington's mediators will know whether the holiday truce can be extended into the kind of comprehensive deal that has eluded earlier attempts this year.
