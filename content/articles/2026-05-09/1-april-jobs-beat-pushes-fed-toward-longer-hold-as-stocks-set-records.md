U.S. employers added 115,000 jobs in April and the unemployment rate held at 4.3 percent, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday, a stronger-than-forecast print that pushed the S&P 500 and Nasdaq composite to record highs and drained what was left of this year's bets on a Federal Reserve rate cut.

The report lands at an awkward moment for incoming Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, whom President Trump has tapped with the expectation he will lower rates. With inflation still above 3 percent and hiring no longer cracking, the case for cuts is shrinking even as the political pressure for them grows.

## What the print said

Nonfarm payrolls rose by a seasonally adjusted 115,000, down from the unusually strong 185,000 added in March but more than double the 55,000 gain forecast in the Dow Jones consensus. Average hourly earnings climbed 0.2 percent for the month and 3.6 percent over the past year, both lower than expected. Healthcare led with 37,000 jobs, followed by transportation and warehousing at 30,000 and retail at 22,000. Information services lost another 13,000 positions, extending a slide that has erased 342,000 jobs, or 11 percent of the category, since November 2022 as artificial-intelligence tools have spread. The March count was revised up by 7,000, but February was cut by 23,000 to a loss of 156,000.

The household survey was less reassuring. The labor force participation rate slipped to 61.8 percent, the lowest since October 2021, and the broader U-6 measure rose 0.2 percentage point to 8.2 percent. The number working part time for economic reasons jumped 445,000 to 4.9 million.

## On the Street

Stocks opened slightly higher and Treasury yields fell, with both the Nasdaq and the S&P 500 touching all-time highs on the back of the print and a wave of strong earnings. Mohamed El-Erian, chief economic adviser at Allianz and a Wharton School professor, told the PBS NewsHour that five companies now account for half of this year's stock-market gains and warned that “there is a geoeconomic ceiling to even this market rally” with the war in Iran still pushing energy prices around.

The bond market read the same data and pulled cuts off the calendar. Fed funds futures now imply essentially no chance of a cut through April 2031, and the curve assigns a higher probability to hikes than to easings over that span.

The message from traders dovetails with the one from inside the building. The Federal Open Market Committee voted 8-4 last week to hold its benchmark rate steady, the most dissents at a single meeting since 1992. The four objectors did not disagree with the decision to hold but with the post-meeting statement's forward guidance, which has been read as tilting toward a cut. Some argued the next move could just as easily be up.

“The Fed will shift its focus to containing upside inflation risks now that the labor market appears back on track,” Lindsay Rosner, head of multisector fixed income at Goldman Sachs Asset Management, said in a note. The FOMC “could well feel compelled to remove the easing bias from its next post-meeting statement in June,” she said.

Austan Goolsbee, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, told CNBC the labor market has been “pretty much stable for a year, year and a half,” and added: “I characterize that we’ve been stable without being good.” The bigger problem, he said, is prices. “We’ve been above the 2% fed target for five years now,” Goolsbee said. “We stopped making progress last year, and now the last three months, it’s going up instead of down.” The consumer price index for March came in at 3.3 percent.

## Warsh's inheritance

The data hands Warsh, who is awaiting Senate confirmation as Jerome Powell's successor, an awkward opening hand. The former Fed governor has argued the central bank can ease policy while still holding inflation in check, and has suggested working through its $6.7 trillion balance sheet rather than the overnight rate. Selling a cut to a committee that just produced four hawkish dissents, with prices rising more than 3 percent a year and payrolls beating expectations, is not the fight he signed up for.

Dan North, senior economist for North America at Allianz, was blunt. “I’m looking through the report trying to find problems, and it’s fairly bulletproof this month,” he said. Of Warsh, he added: “Certainly he was chosen by Trump because he is probably leaning towards lower interest rates.”

## The counterpoint

The day's reporting came almost entirely from the financial wires; the White House had not publicly commented on the print by the time markets closed, and partisan outlets had yet to shape the political read. The cleanest dissent is inside the data itself. The household survey showed 226,000 fewer workers and a participation rate at a four-and-a-half-year low, and a University of Michigan consumer sentiment reading released the same day fell to a record low for the second straight month, down nearly 8 percent from a year earlier. “The consumer is quite a paradox, because they say they're feeling awful, and yet they continue to spend,” El-Erian told PBS, adding that the pattern “cannot continue indefinitely” for lower-income households. Black unemployment has climbed to roughly twice the white rate, and Hispanic unemployment also worsened in April.

The Fed's next policy decision is due in June, when Warsh, if confirmed, would face his first test on whether to drop the easing bias from the committee's statement.
