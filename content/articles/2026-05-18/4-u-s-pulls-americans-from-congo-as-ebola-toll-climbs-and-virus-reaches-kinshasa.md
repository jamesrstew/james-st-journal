The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said Sunday it is coordinating the withdrawal of a small number of Americans from the Ebola outbreak zone in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, where at least six U.S. citizens have been exposed to the Bundibugyo virus and one is symptomatic, sources with international aid organizations told CBS News. The disclosure came as the suspected death toll rose to 87 and the WHO confirmed a laboratory-positive case in Kinshasa, roughly 1,000 kilometers from the Ituri epicenter.

The withdrawal and the Kinshasa case mark the first concrete signs that an outbreak the World Health Organization declared a public health emergency of international concern on Sunday is escaping its initial geography. Three of the exposed Americans had high-risk contact with the virus, the CBS News sources said.

## What the CDC said

The CDC said it is supporting interagency partners "who are actively coordinating the safe withdrawal of a small number of Americans who are directly affected by this outbreak." The agency said "the risk to the American public remains low" and issued travel advisories urging enhanced precautions in Congo and Uganda. CDC Ebola response manager Satish Pillai declined Sunday to discuss the affected Americans, saying only that the agency was "assessing the needs on the ground," NBC News reported.

The Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention on Saturday tallied 336 suspected cases and 87 deaths in Congo, plus two cases in Uganda involving patients who had traveled from Congo. One died May 14 at Kibuli Muslim Hospital in Kampala.

## A wider footprint

The Kinshasa case, disclosed by the WHO on X, involved a patient who had visited Ituri. Local officials in Goma, the eastern city held by the M23 rebel group, separately confirmed one case there. Congo on Sunday closed its land border with Rwanda, the State Department said.

There is no approved vaccine or treatment for the Bundibugyo strain, which has caused only two previous outbreaks since it was identified in Uganda in 2007. Africa CDC Director-General Dr. Jean Kaseya told Sky News on Sunday he was in "panic mode" over the shortage of medicines and vaccines. The outbreak's origin remains unidentified, Kaseya said: "This outbreak started in April. So far, we don't know the index case. It means we don't know how far is the magnitude of this outbreak."

Doctors Without Borders said it was preparing a "large-scale response." Trish Newport, the group's emergency program manager, said in a statement carried by NBC News that "the number of cases and deaths we are seeing in such a short timeframe, combined with the spread across several health zones and now across the border, is extremely concerning."

## The counterpoint

The WHO has been explicit that the outbreak does not meet the threshold of a pandemic emergency on the order of COVID-19 and has advised against closing international borders. Kenya's government said Saturday it assessed only a "moderate risk of importation" from regional travel. Ebola spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids, not casual contact or air, and the CDC's public-risk assessment for Americans at home has not changed.

A team of 35 WHO and Congolese Health Ministry experts arrived in Bunia, Ituri's capital, on Sunday with 7 tons of emergency medical supplies, the WHO's Africa office said.
