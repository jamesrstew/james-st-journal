U.K. gilt yields steadied on Monday after last week's sell-off, and Greater Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham spent the weekend trying to reassure bond traders that he would not blow up Britain's fiscal rules if he replaces Prime Minister Keir Starmer, who has so far refused to resign.

The 10-year gilt yield eased 2 basis points to 5.15 percent as London opened, and the 30-year, which closed Friday at its highest level since 1998, slipped 2 basis points to 5.83 percent, according to CNBC. The pause arrives in the second week of a leadership crisis that has cost Starmer two ministers, produced no formal challenger, and turned every signal from a possible successor into a gilt-market event.

## What Burnham said

Burnham, the Labour left's most prominent figure outside Westminster, used an ITV News interview at the weekend to row back from earlier comments that Britain was "in hock to the bond markets."

"I have never said you can just ignore the bond markets," Burnham told the broadcaster. "I said politicians had placed Britain in hock because of the way in which we lost control of our finances and public spending when we handed away control of energy, water, housing."

Deutsche Bank analysts told clients Monday that the clarification was unlikely to settle the question. Investors, they wrote, "are likely to fear higher fiscal spending with Burnham as PM."

## The by-election problem

Burnham cannot challenge Starmer until he sits in the House of Commons. Labour has cleared him to contest a by-election in Makerfield, in the north of England, that the BBC has reported will be held June 18. The seat is marginal. Nigel Farage's Reform UK polled strongly there at this month's local elections, and Deutsche Bank told clients that "[m]uch will depend on how aggressively the Green Party contests the seat and splits the left-wing vote." Burnham's pre-2016 support for remaining in the European Union is expected to feature in the campaign in a constituency that voted Leave.

Carsten Nickel, managing director at Teneo, told CNBC that Brexit was likely to be used for "short-term politicking" rather than as a guide to longer-term policy. What the markets actually wanted, he said, was an answer the candidates have yet to provide: "The only thing I'd be interested [to hear] at this stage would be a longer-term debate in terms of where do [they] want to head as the Labour Party? Like some progressive consensus at the center, but that's not there."

## The premium

Lizzie Galbraith, senior political economist at Aberdeen, said Monday that an "extra risk premia" had been priced into gilts on top of the global bond rout. The reason, she told CNBC, was time. "We don't yet have a leadership contest underway," Galbraith said, "[but] we're potentially looking at months here of policy debate, policy uncertainty, and markets trying to predict where the Labour Party may move if we do get that eventual leadership challenge."

Alongside Burnham, former Health Secretary Wes Streeting and former Deputy Prime Minister Angela Rayner remain the names most often cited as possible challengers. None has lodged a formal bid.

## The other view

Al Jazeera's Andrew Marshall, writing Monday, argued that the most consequential fact in the saga is what has not happened: Starmer "has not even faced a formal challenge to his leadership yet," and no sitting Labour prime minister ever has. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch told the Commons last week that "[t]he PM has shown he is in office but not in power" — a deliberate echo of Norman Lamont's 1993 line about John Major, who went on to lead his party for nearly four more years. Labour rules require 20 percent of MPs to endorse a challenger before a contest can begin, a higher bar than the Conservatives use and one that has historically protected incumbents.

The next fixed date on the calendar is June 18, when voters in Makerfield will decide whether the man hedge funds are betting against gets the seat he needs to make his move.
