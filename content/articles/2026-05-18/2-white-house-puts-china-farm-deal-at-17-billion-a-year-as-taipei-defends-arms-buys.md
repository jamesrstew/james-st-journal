The White House on Sunday put a dollar figure on the farm-purchase commitment President Trump brought home from Beijing, saying China had agreed to buy at least $17 billion of U.S. agricultural goods annually through 2028 and to address American access to four rare-earth minerals. Hours earlier in Taipei, Taiwanese President Lai Ching-te defended his government's U.S. arms purchases after Trump publicly wavered on continued support for the island following his return from China.

The twin developments turned last week's summit, which the two sides had announced in broad strokes on Friday, into a concrete deliverable on agriculture and a fresh diplomatic test on Taiwan. Grain markets jumped on the readout. Taiwanese officials moved to lock in U.S. backing before Trump decides whether to sign a $14 billion arms package that Beijing opposed in the run-up to the meetings.

## What was new

The $17 billion-a-year figure is the first hard number the administration has attached to the deal since Trump and Xi Jinping concluded two days of meetings in Beijing on Friday. The White House said the commitment runs through 2028 and is, in its words, "in addition to the soybean purchase commitments that it made in October 2025." After a Trump-Xi meeting in South Korea last fall, the U.S. said China had agreed to buy at least 25 million metric tons of American soybeans in each of the following three years.

The weekend readout did not specify a fresh soybean tonnage, while stating China is again allowing sales of U.S. beef and poultry. The U.S. side also said China would address shortages of yttrium, scandium, neodymium and indium, the obscure minerals used in smartphones, cars and weapons whose supply chains Beijing largely controls.

China's Commerce Ministry, in its own readout, did not specify an amount or name soybeans, noted only that both countries agreed to promote agricultural trade, and did not mention rare earths at all. Beijing indicated tariff reductions would be part of the plans; the U.S. statement did not mention duties.

## Taipei pushes back

Lai, in a Sunday statement, called U.S. arms sales to Taiwan "the most important deterrent" of regional conflict and instability. He thanked Trump "for his continued support for peace and stability across the Taiwan Strait since his first term, including the continuous increase in the scale and amount of arms sales to Taiwan," framing the relationship as both legally governed and a catalyst for regional peace.

The statement followed remarks Trump made after leaving Beijing in which he called into question continued U.S. support for Taiwan and said he had not yet decided whether to sign off on the pending $14 billion arms package that Beijing had pressed him to drop. The package still needs his signature.

## The Boeing line

Both readouts also confirmed an aircraft order. The U.S. side specified Chinese plans to buy 200 Boeing airplanes; Beijing broadly noted the aircraft purchase agreement and said the U.S. would ensure supply of engines and other parts. China has developed its own passenger airplane, which still relies on foreign-made parts. The two governments said they would establish boards of trade and investment to handle further bilateral talks.

## The skeptical view

Jacob Shapiro, strategic partner and geopolitical advisor at The Bespoke Group, called the summit itself "underwhelming" on CNBC's "Squawk Box Asia" on Monday and said U.S.-China relations would likely improve only "incrementally" as long as Trump is president. He argued Beijing would "say what they need to say to make things nice for the next couple of years" while preparing for a successor likely to take a harsher line. Beijing had not publicly contested the U.S. dollar figure by press time, and Chinese state media had not put out an alternative framing of the same numbers; the available wire and broadcast reporting Sunday came from U.S. and Western outlets.

Trump and Xi are set to meet again in the U.S. in September, with a state visit to the White House on the calendar for Sept. 24.
