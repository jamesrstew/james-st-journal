Iran on Monday rejected the framing that an agreement with the United States to end the 12-week war was about to be signed, with foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baqaei telling reporters in Tehran that talks remained snagged on what he called 'problems and obstacles' caused by shifting American positions. President Trump, who said over the weekend that a pact had been 'largely negotiated,' wrote Monday morning that he had instructed his representatives 'not to rush into a deal.'

The pullback widened the political fight in Washington over the emerging terms, with Republican senators attacking the draft and Secretary of State Marco Rubio publicly raising the prospect of 'another way' should diplomacy collapse. Regional officials told the Associated Press on Sunday the sides were close to a memorandum that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz, end the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports and commit Tehran to give up its 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 percent purity.

## What Iran said

Baqaei told a weekly news briefing that negotiators had 'reached a conclusion on a large portion of the issues under discussion,' but added: 'to say that this means the signing of an agreement is imminent — no one can make such a claim.' He said Iran had 'not set any timeline or specific time for finalizing' a deal and accused Washington of contradicting itself in public.

He described the document as a 14-point memorandum focused on ending the war and on a staged reopening of the Strait of Hormuz over a 60-day window, during which Washington would lift its blockade and Tehran would lift its closure of the waterway. Nuclear questions, he said, would be discussed during that window rather than resolved upfront. Iran has not publicly committed to surrendering its highly enriched uranium, a central Trump demand.

On a separate sticking point, Baqaei pushed back on U.S. accusations that Iran intends to charge tolls for shipping through the strait. 'Iran does not charge tolls. There is no toll,' he said, while adding that vessels would have to pay for 'navigation services' and environmental protection.

## Trump pulls back

Trump's Monday post on his social media platform reversed the urgency of his Sunday messaging. 'Both sides must take their time and get it right. There can be no mistakes,' he wrote, repeating that the U.S. blockade 'will remain in full force and effect until an agreement is reached, certified, and signed.' He framed any pact as 'THE EXACT OPPOSITE' of the 2015 Obama-era nuclear agreement, which he withdrew from in his first term.

A senior administration official told CBS News that negotiators have agreed to broad principles, including in-principle Iranian agreement to dispose of its highly enriched uranium; a U.S. official told the Associated Press the proposal contemplates a 60-day window and that sanctions relief is conditional on Iran surrendering its stockpile, which Russia has offered to take. Rubio, speaking in New Delhi, said an announcement could come 'today' but cautioned reporters not to read too much into the timing, and added that Washington would either get a good agreement or deal with Iran 'another way.'

## Republicans split

The sharpest opposition came from inside Trump's own party. Sen. Ted Cruz of Texas wrote on X that allowing an Iranian government 'still run by Islamists who chant death to America' to receive sanctions relief, keep enrichment capability and retain leverage over the Strait of Hormuz 'would be a disastrous mistake.' Sen. Roger Wicker of Mississippi, who chairs the Armed Services Committee, called the proposed 60-day ceasefire window a 'disaster' and said 'Everything accomplished by Operation Epic Fury would be for naught!' Mike Pompeo, Trump's first-term secretary of state, said the draft looked like the Obama-era deal and was 'Not remotely America First.'

Sen. Lindsey Graham of South Carolina urged Trump to 'stick to his guns' and pressed for Gulf states to join the Abraham Accords as part of any settlement. Sen. Rand Paul of Kentucky defended the president, writing that 'War virtually always ends with negotiations.'

## Democratic objection

Democrats raised a different complaint: that the deal trades U.S. concessions for a return to a status quo the war itself disrupted. Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland told CBS News that 'when you're digging a hole, you should stop digging,' arguing the United States would be agreeing to reopen a strait that was already open before the February attack on Iran. Polls cited by the Associated Press show the war is unpopular; the operation has cost taxpayers at least $29 billion and killed 13 U.S. service members.

## Sticking points

Iran disputed Trump's characterization that the strait would be 'reopened' under U.S. terms, telling the semi-official Fars News Agency that 'in the event of a possible agreement, the Strait of Hormuz will still be under Iranian management.' Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, after a Sunday call with Trump, said any final agreement must require 'dismantling Iran's nuclear enrichment sites' — a harder condition than the 60-day discussion window Tehran has described.

Pakistan's army chief, who has been mediating, was in Beijing on Monday, and Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif said Islamabad hoped to host the next round 'very soon.' Whether a memorandum is signed in that round, or whether Rubio's 'another way' begins to take shape, is likely to be settled within the 60-day window both sides keep invoking but neither has agreed to start.
