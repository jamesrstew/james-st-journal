Treasury yields fell across the curve Tuesday as bond traders returned from the Memorial Day break and bet that a U.S.-Iran peace framework would hold, even after U.S. Central Command carried out what it called "self defense" strikes in southern Iran early in the day. The 10-year note yield slipped more than 6 basis points to 4.510%, the 2-year fell more than 6 basis points to 4.066%, and the 30-year bond eased more than 5 basis points to 5.028%. The relief rally did not erase the bigger signal sitting underneath it: a $145 trillion global bond market that, in the words of Axios, is telling rich-country governments the era of the free lunch is over.

The stakes run from the Levittown closing table to the West Wing. Higher long yields raise mortgage rates for American homebuyers and borrowing costs for U.S. companies in the near term, and over the medium term they hand Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh a thornier set of tradeoffs than any of his predecessors faced in the 2010s. Warsh, sworn in Friday, has signaled a higher-for-longer policy stance just as the long end of the curve is repricing fiscal risk on its own.

## Yields and the tape

The 30-year Treasury bond yielded 5.06 at Friday's close, Axios reported, after touching a post-2007 high of 5.18% earlier in the week and rising from 4.63% at the end of February. Tuesday's pullback brought the long bond back to 5.028% as European sovereign yields, which had dropped sharply Monday on Hormuz-thaw optimism, gave back some ground.

## What traders are pricing

"Bond markets are pricing the new geoeconomic reality," Daleep Singh, the chief global economist at PGIM, told Axios. "In a world of intensified geopolitical rivalry — where economics has become the main arena of competition — supply-side shocks are going to keep coming." Tuesday's tape carried that argument in miniature. President Trump posted on TruthSocial that negotiations with Tehran were "proceeding nicely," while Secretary of State Marco Rubio, traveling in India, said the Strait of Hormuz would have to be opened "one way or the other." Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it had engaged U.S. drones and an F-35 over Iranian airspace and warned of retaliation for ceasefire violations.

## The other side

The end-of-free-lunch thesis is a bond-market interpretation, not a consensus. Neither the Treasury Department nor the White House offered a public response to the long-end selloff by press time, and no fiscal-policy economist arguing the move reflects term premium normalization, rather than a structural break, appeared in the day's reporting. Singh's geopolitical framing is one read; others tie the steepening to Warsh's hawkish opening posture and rebuilding inflation risk premia.

The next test arrives later this week, when the Commerce Department releases April personal consumption expenditures, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge. Bank of America forecasts a 0.4% monthly rise and a 3.8% year-on-year headline reading. A print at or above that level would test whether Tuesday's rally is the start of a durable bid for Treasuries or a one-day reprieve in a market that, as Axios put it, has decided American lawmakers can no longer order an "all-candy, no-spinach fiscal diet."
