World markets tumbled Friday as a global selloff in artificial-intelligence and chip stocks widened, with Tokyo's Nikkei 225 losing 4 percent, Taiwan's benchmark falling 6.5 percent and futures on the S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average pointing to a lower open, after Netflix's Thursday-night earnings miss and a fresh Chinese AI model gave investors two more reasons to question stretched valuations across the AI trade.

The rout carried the AI complex from a story about limitless demand into one about limits. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.'s Thursday pledge of another $100 billion for U.S. fabrication plants, meant to signal exactly the kind of demand skeptics have doubted, instead sent its own Taipei-listed shares down 6.5 percent Friday. “That tells you the AI trade isn’t being priced on growth anymore. It’s being priced on perfection,” an investment executive told Semafor.

## Asia leads down

South Korean markets were closed and did not trade Friday. In early European trading, Germany's DAX slipped 0.6 percent to 24,774.69, France's CAC 40 fell 0.7 percent to 8,318.74 and Britain's FTSE 100 edged 0.1 percent lower to 10,560.93. Futures on the S&P 500 dropped 0.8 percent and futures on the Dow lost 0.6 percent.

## A new China moment

Adding to the pressure was Friday's release by the Chinese startup Moonshot of Kimi K3, a new open-source AI model that echoed the early-2025 DeepSeek episode in which a Chinese low-cost rival briefly wiped hundreds of billions of dollars from U.S. chip valuations. The Associated Press said the launch “further shook up markets” and revived worries that cheaper models could sap demand for the high-end processors and memory chips around which the U.S. AI buildout has been designed.

## TSMC's welcome that wasn't

TSMC's Thursday announcement — an additional $100 billion for its Arizona fabs alongside record second-quarter earnings, both positive on their face — landed inside a tape already skeptical of AI capital spending. The Wall Street Journal, cited by Semafor, wrote that investors were unnerved by “stretched valuations and ever-high expectations.” Chip stocks have shown “meaningful cracks” in recent weeks, an analyst told Bloomberg, and “will raise some real warning flags” without a swift rebound. The TSMC pledge itself, this newspaper's Thursday lead, had already been the market's headline event before Friday's selling swept it into the rout.

## Netflix miss adds fuel

Netflix compounded the mood after Thursday's close. The streaming company reported second-quarter revenue of $12.56 billion, up 13 percent from a year earlier but just short of analyst estimates, and net income of $3.40 billion, or 80 cents a share, compared with $3.13 billion, or 72 cents, in the same quarter last year. Shares fell more than 8 percent in after-hours trading as investors focused on a 2026 revenue outlook narrowed to $51 billion to $51.4 billion, from earlier guidance of $50.7 billion to $51.7 billion, and third-quarter revenue growth guided at 12 percent.

Netflix also said it would publish its “What We Watched” engagement report only once a year beginning in 2027, starting with the first quarter, rather than alongside earnings. “I'll start by saying there is not a linear relationship between viewing hours and revenue and profit, because all hours are not created equal,” co-CEO Greg Peters told analysts. Co-CEO Ted Sarandos disputed reports of falling second-season viewership. “Our season two fall off has actually slightly improved this year relative to last year, so no changes in release strategies,” Sarandos said. Chief Financial Officer Spencer Neumann said Netflix remained “primarily builders, not buyers,” months after the company walked away from a bid for Warner Bros. Discovery's film and streaming assets.

Netflix expects to roughly double advertising revenue this year to $3 billion, with U.S. Upfront discussions in “advanced stages” and commitments expected in the coming weeks. Live sports — Women's World Cup matches, additional NFL games, MLB events and WWE — accounted for six of the top 10 new-member sign-up days over the past five years, the company said, even though live programming makes up about 1 percent of total viewing hours.

## The counterpoint

Wire-service reporters covering the tape were the only voices on hand by press time; Friday's selloff drew no notable defense from the buy-side names that have championed the AI trade. Bulls would argue that TSMC's willingness to commit an additional $100 billion of capital is itself the strongest possible signal that end-demand for AI compute remains intact, and that Netflix's slower growth reflects the mechanics of subscriber saturation rather than a broader consumption slowdown.

## What to watch

The next test lands Wednesday, when Tesla and Alphabet report second-quarter results, followed by Microsoft, Meta and Amazon later in the month — a run of prints that will together determine whether the AI capital-spending story, so far the bull case's only pillar, still holds.
