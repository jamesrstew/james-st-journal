Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix and Micron Technology rebounded in early Wednesday trading after a one-day rout in artificial-intelligence stocks erased roughly $1.3 trillion in market value across three continents, leaving investors to decide whether the worst selloff of the year was a pause after a record run or the first crack in the capital-spending case for AI.

The Nasdaq composite fell 2.2 percent on Tuesday and the S&P 500 dropped 1.4 percent to 7,365.46 as semiconductor and memory-chip shares led losses, according to AP. South Korea's Kospi, which has nearly doubled in 2026, closed 10 percent lower and triggered a trading halt before reopening Wednesday up more than 3 percent. The swings put the sustainability of more than $720 billion in planned AI infrastructure spending by Alphabet, Amazon, Meta Platforms and Microsoft squarely back in front of the market.

## What shifted

The selling that began in Seoul on Monday rolled into the New York session Tuesday and concentrated in the names most exposed to AI data-center construction. Micron Technology slumped 13.2 percent and Nvidia fell 4.1 percent, AP reported. Intel, Advanced Micro Devices and Qualcomm each lost more than 5 percent, according to CNBC. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index slid and the iShares Semiconductor ETF dropped 7.9 percent. The Invesco QQQ Trust fell 3.3 percent.

Memory and storage names took the hardest hits. Sandisk, whose shares have risen more than 700 percent this year, sank 13.6 percent. Marvell Technologies, which turned its first annual profit in five years, lost 9.4 percent. By Wednesday's premarket, Micron was up 3.63 percent and Sandisk up 2.52 percent, CNBC reported, and the Roundhill Memory ETF had recovered 3.7 percent of the prior session's 14 percent loss.

## The capex question

The immediate trigger was rate-path math. Wall Street now sees an 85 percent chance the Federal Reserve will raise its benchmark rate before year-end, up from 60 percent a week earlier, according to CME Group data cited by AP. A Thursday reading of the Fed's preferred inflation gauge is expected to show prices rose 4.1 percent in May. The yield on the 10-year Treasury slipped to 4.50 percent from 4.51 percent.

Behind the rate worry sits a larger question about the cash going into data centers. Alphabet said this month it is raising $80 billion by selling stock to help fund as much as $190 billion in 2026 investments, with spending forecast to "significantly increase" next year, AP reported. Amazon sold $54 billion of bonds in March to underwrite roughly $200 billion in AI outlays. SpaceX, which went public on June 12, has said part of an upcoming bond offering will fund its AI buildout. Its shares wavered Tuesday, briefly trading below the $150 IPO price before closing at about $156, according to the BBC.

Valuations have moved faster than profits. Marvell's price-to-earnings ratio has climbed from about 30 at the start of the year to near 100. Sandisk's stands at 68. The S&P 500's is around 25.

## On the Street

Analysts split on what the session meant. Dan Ives of Wedbush Securities, in a research note, said channel checks across Asia and enterprise AI demand showed "no cracks in the armor" and called the Kospi drop a pause after the index's near-doubling. Bank of America's Vivek Arya, the BBC reported, told clients that sticky inflation and strengthening demand will push sector forecasts higher and that chipmakers are moving from defending returns on investment to confronting power and infrastructure constraints.

Other voices were more cautious. Brock Weimer, an investment strategy analyst at Edward Jones, wrote that "a period of consolidation is reasonable, in our view, after such a sharp move higher" and attributed Tuesday's drop to profit-taking with no clear catalyst. Philip Straehl, chief investment officer at Morningstar Wealth, warned in a report last week that "periods of elevated capital investment have historically not translated into strong outcomes for investors," with semiconductors "particularly exposed."

## The counterpoint

The AI-spending companies did not respond publicly Tuesday, and the wires that drove the day's framing — AP, the BBC, CNBC and Axios — converged on the same center-of-the-road read: a sharp pullback after a 90-day rally, with the bull and bear cases both still on the table. No left- or right-leaning outlet drove a distinctive frame on the rout, and the hyperscalers' chief financial officers, who have defended capex on recent earnings calls, did not amend their guidance.

London's FTSE 100 stayed in positive territory through Tuesday's session, Danni Hewson, head of financial analysis at AJ Bell, told the BBC, because the index carries relatively few technology shares.

## What's next

Thursday's personal-consumption-expenditures inflation report is the next test. Second-quarter earnings from the hyperscalers begin in three weeks and will mark the first time investors hear from Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft since the selloff put their combined $720 billion in 2026 AI commitments under fresh scrutiny.
