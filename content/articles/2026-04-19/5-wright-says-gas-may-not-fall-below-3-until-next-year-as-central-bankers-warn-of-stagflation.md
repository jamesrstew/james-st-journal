Energy Secretary Chris Wright said Sunday that U.S. gasoline prices may not fall back below $3 a gallon until next year, hours after Iran fired on two tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz and two days after Tehran had briefly declared the waterway reopened to commercial traffic.

The timeline pushes sub-$3 gas into 2027 and lands as central bankers gathered at the International Monetary Fund's spring meetings in Washington described a fog of war that is forcing them to hold rate decisions meeting-to-meeting. Regular unleaded averaged $4.04 a gallon over the weekend, according to AAA, up from $2.90 on Feb. 1, the day before the war with Iran began.

## What Wright said

Speaking on CNN's "State of the Union," Wright was asked when prices at the pump would return to pre-war figures. "I don't know, that could happen later this year, that might not happen until next year, but prices have likely peaked," he said. "Certainly with a resolution of this conflict, energy prices will go down."

Wright added that "under $3 a gallon is pretty tremendous in inflation-adjusted terms," and said the U.S. would "get back there for sure." U.S. envoys are scheduled to meet Iranian counterparts in Islamabad on Monday for the next round of talks, and oil prices tumbled late last week on signals that the strait was reopening before Iran fired on the tankers over the weekend.

## On the rate path

At the IMF meetings, Bank of France Governor François Villeroy de Galhau told CNBC that policymakers "cannot bet only on the most favorable scenario" and that the European Central Bank expects "higher inflation and we expect lower growth." Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel, an ECB Governing Council member, called the situation "very opaque, very cloudy" and said officials had moved to a "meeting-to-meeting approach" with the next decision due in two weeks.

Pierre Gramegna, managing director of the European Stability Mechanism, laid out a concrete inflation path: if the Strait of Hormuz stays "blocked or half-blocked" for another couple of months, euro-zone inflation could run "more than 1%, maybe 1.5% this year." A longer disruption, he said, would push that figure to 2.5 percent and "trigger probably stagflation."

## The counterparty

Greek Finance Minister Kyriakos Pierrakakis warned CNBC the world is "potentially looking at the greatest energy crisis in history," noting that about a third of global fertilizer shipments, plus sulfur, helium and petrochemicals, pass through the strait. New Zealand Finance Minister Nicola Willis said her government was preparing for a "worst-case scenario" in which crude is trapped in the Middle East and cannot reach refineries in Southeast Asia.

Both pieces of reporting reviewed for this article were from CNBC, a center-lean outlet; no left- or right-leaning cross-reporting on Wright's remarks or the IMF-sidelines interviews had been published by press time, and administration officials offered no public counter to the central bankers' stagflation warnings.

The next test comes Monday in Islamabad, where U.S. and Iranian negotiators resume talks, with the pump price and the ECB's May meeting both waiting on the outcome.
