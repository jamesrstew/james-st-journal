A humanoid robot built by Chinese smartphone maker Honor finished a 21-kilometer half-marathon in Beijing on Sunday in 50 minutes and 26 seconds, running an average of about 25 kilometers per hour and crossing the line roughly seven minutes ahead of the men's world record of 57:20 set last month by Ugandan runner Jacob Kiplimo.

The performance, staged on a parallel course to about 12,000 human competitors in the southern Yizhuang district, marks the first time a bipedal machine has outrun the fastest humans over a recognized road-race distance, and lands at a moment when Beijing is pouring state and private capital into humanoid robotics as part of a broader technology contest with the United States.

## What shifted

The winning robot was equipped with an autonomous navigation system, according to Chinese state broadcaster CCTV. Organizers said nearly half of the dozens of humanoids entered ran without remote operators, while the rest were teleoperated. The field has expanded from roughly 20 machines at last year's inaugural race, when the top finisher needed more than two hours and 40 minutes and robots repeatedly fell, to more than 100 entries this year.

Engineers on the Honor team said the winning machine was designed to mimic elite athletes, with legs measuring about 37 inches and a liquid-cooling system intended to hold performance over the 13-mile distance. "Looking ahead, some of these technologies might be transferred to other areas," Du Xiaodi, an engineer on the Honor team, told reporters. "For example, structural reliability and liquid-cooling technology could be applied in future industrial scenarios."

The race was not flawless. Fox News, citing organizers, reported that some robots stumbled at the start and others veered into barriers, and the two lanes were kept separate to avoid collisions between the machines and human runners.

## The money behind it

Chinese investment in robotics and so-called embodied artificial intelligence reached 73.5 billion yuan, or about $10.8 billion, in 2025, according to a government study cited by Al Jazeera. The Beijing event is pitched as a showcase to accelerate that spending, and several Chinese firms used Sunday's race to market hardware aimed at factory, logistics and home-service work.

The commercial picture is narrower than the marketing. Reuters, cited by Fox News, reported that widespread industrial deployment of humanoids remains limited and that Chinese developers are still working to build the artificial-intelligence software needed for the machines to match the efficiency of human factory workers. A marathon course, flat and unobstructed, is a controlled demonstration rather than a factory floor.

## On the sidelines

Spectators behind safety barriers described a mix of wonder and disquiet. Sun Zhigang, who attended with his son, told the Associated Press, "It's the first time robots have surpassed humans, and that's something I never imagined." Han Chenyu, a 25-year-old student, told Agence France-Presse the event was "pretty cool" but added, "as someone who works for a living, I'm a little worried about it sometimes. I feel like technology is advancing so fast that it might start affecting people's jobs."

Xie Lei, 41, watching with his family, said robots could "become part of our daily lives" within a few years, potentially handling "things like housework, elderly companionship or basic caregiving" or "dangerous jobs, even firefighting."

## The counterpoint

Today's reporting comes from Chinese state media and two partisan outlets, with no on-the-ground account from a U.S. or European wire service. The 50:26 figure has not been certified by World Athletics, which governs human road-running records, and the robot ran in a segregated lane under conditions tailored to its hardware. Prior humanoid demonstrations from both Chinese and American developers have produced viral single-event results that did not translate into reliable commercial products. The Honor team declined to detail the rate of human intervention, if any, during the race.

U.S. officials have meanwhile moved to restrict Chinese robotics on national-security grounds, Fox News noted, one sign that Washington views Sunday's spectacle as more than a sporting curiosity. The next test for the field is whether the liquid-cooling and navigation stack Du described can survive a workday rather than a weekend on a closed course.
