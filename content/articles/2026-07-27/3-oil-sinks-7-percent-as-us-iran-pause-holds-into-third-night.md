Brent crude sank more than 7 percent to below $88 a barrel Monday and U.S. equity futures pointed higher, as a pause in U.S.-Iran hostilities held for a third consecutive night and traders unwound the war premium built up over two weeks of strikes.

The retreat handed drivers, importers and rate-setting central banks their first real breather since the fighting resumed this month. It did not restore normal traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the channel that carried a fifth of the world's oil before the conflict — which Iran's foreign ministry said Monday remains "still closed." Tankers bound for Asia are still detouring around the Cape of Good Hope, and the Bank of England meets this week with inflation risks tilted higher, not lower.

## What shifted

Brent, which settled above $100 last Thursday for the first time since May, was trading below $88 in Monday's session. The BBC reported an intraday drop of more than 9 percent from Friday's close; Semafor and CNBC put the two-day decline at more than 7 percent. Futures markets pointed to strong performance on Wall Street, CNBC reported, and the dollar softened alongside crude as the war premium began to bleed out.

## The pause

The U.S. military halted two weeks of strikes Friday to give peace talks "some space." Iran, which had been striking U.S. bases in the region, said it will stop launching strikes as long as the U.S. does the same. Iran's foreign ministry spokesperson, Esmail Baghaei, said Monday that Tehran "currently have no negotiations with the United States," and reiterated that the only official talks are with Oman on the future of the Strait of Hormuz. An Omani delegation was in Tehran on Friday and Saturday. Baghaei called them "useful discussions."

## Shipping's long way around

The tape under crude is not calm. Houthi attacks in the Red Sea are pushing tankers to reroute around Africa, Semafor reported, and Saudi Arabia's exports to Asia are increasingly dependent on the Suez Canal or the longer Cape route. Deutsche Bank analysts wrote in a Monday note: "The main market risk remains the energy and shipping front," adding that "Traffic through Hormuz remains severely disrupted, while the conflict has broadened into the Red Sea." The Saudi military struck Iran-backed Houthi targets in Yemen over the weekend, and Ukraine separately said it hit an Iranian commercial vessel in the Caspian Sea. Semafor, citing the New York Times, reported that the U.S. military is running short of key ordnance and that President Trump's offensive options are limited.

## Central banks in the middle

Central banks are pricing a two-track story. The European Central Bank raised rates in June for the first time in nearly three years, citing "inflation pressures" from the conflict. Traders no longer expect Bank of England cuts this year and are pricing a rate rise by year-end, the BBC reported. The Bank of England is expected to hold at 3.75 percent when it meets this week.

## The counterpoint

The retreat is being priced as adaptability, not peace. Susannah Streeter of Wealth Club told the BBC markets remain "cautious given the twists and turns during this conflict" and warned that "there is still significant uncertainty baked into these prices and a reticence about whether negotiations will lead to a lasting breakthrough," pointing to the possibility of another flare-up. Inside the administration, one U.S. official told Semafor there is "a mix of opinions" on next steps, adding that "The problem is, [there are] no good options." Iran denied weekend media reports it had agreed to a 10-day ceasefire, and Baghaei said the Strait's status has "not changed and it is still closed."

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu left for Washington on Monday for talks with President Trump on Iran, and the Bank of England releases its rate decision this week. Both will test whether the pause becomes a settlement — or just an interval.
